## Overview

Plants and animals operate on the same excitation to redox architecture. Both kingdoms generate reactive oxygen species through **stress** and through **persistent foreign object intrusion**, and both produce the same ROS species through identical calcium linked biochemical logic.

Stress increases intracellular calcium in both kingdoms. In animals, stress enhances glutamate receptor activity, increases calcium influx, and elevates mitochondrial production of superoxide and hydrogen peroxide [(Khan et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.14336/AD.2024.0125-1); [(Wu et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41401-025-01576-w). In plants, stress activates GLR channels and RBOH enzymes, raising superoxide and hydrogen peroxide through calcium dependent oxidation of NADPH [(Marino et al., 2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tplants.2011.10.001); [(Suzuki et al., 2011)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pbi.2011.07.014). Both kingdoms therefore produce identical ROS species through the same calcium coupled pathways, creating a shared baseline of stress induced oxidative activity.

Persistent foreign objects create the second route, and the parallel between kingdoms is exact. In animals, macrophages surround an unremovable object such as asbestos, silica, or a persistent microbial fragment. They attempt to engulf it but fail, which creates a stable inflammatory ring of activated cells around the object. This ring produces continuous ROS and drives the kynurenine pathway toward quinolinic acid, a strong NMDA receptor agonist that amplifies calcium influx and intensifies oxidative load.

In plants, insects, nematodes, microbes, or injected effectors remain embedded in tissue and cannot be removed. The surrounding plant cells form a gall that encloses the intruder, creating a structural ring of activated tissue that directly parallels the inflammatory ring in animals. This gall tissue produces elevated auxin, which amplifies GLR mediated calcium signaling and drives RBOH dependent ROS production. Both containment structures maintain chronic excitatory activation and raise ROS through identical calcium linked mechanisms.

Quinolinic acid in animals and auxin in plants are therefore ligand amplifiers that perform equivalent functions. Both raise excitatory signaling, increase calcium throughput, intensify ROS generation, and push tissue toward oxidative strain. When this excitatory load exceeds mitochondrial tolerance, both kingdoms adopt a glycolytic survival strategy. In animals this produces the early tumor microenvironment. In plants this produces gall tissue. Tumors and galls are parallel hypergrowth structures stabilized by identical redox and ligand driven processes.

This establishes a single conserved excitatory redox mechanism across plants and animals.

## GLR and Glutamate Receptor Parallels

GLR channels in plants and NMDA and AMPA receptors in animals are functional equivalents. Both mediate calcium entry during activation and link excitation to ROS generation. In plants, GLR channels regulate hydrogen peroxide signaling during cold and salt stress and support acclimation through calcium dependent redox activity [(Li et al., 2019)](https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.13623). GLR mediated nitric oxide signaling also preserves antioxidant balance under salt stress [(Gokce et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10265-024-01528-1).

In animals, glutamate receptor activation increases calcium influx, drives mitochondrial respiration, and generates superoxide and hydrogen peroxide during excitatory states [(Khan et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.14336/AD.2024.0125-1). The structural and functional mapping between GLRs and glutamate receptors defines a direct plant to animal correspondence in excitatory redox coupling and makes the auxin to quinolinic acid parallel coherent across kingdoms.

## RBOH and Mitochondrial ROS Amplification

Plants and animals use similar machinery to amplify ROS once calcium rises.

Plants use respiratory burst oxidase homolog enzymes. These enzymes convert NADPH into superoxide and hydrogen peroxide in a calcium dependent manner and act as central ROS amplifiers in all major stress responses [(Marino et al., 2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tplants.2011.10.001). RBOH integrates phosphorylation and calcium inputs to regulate the magnitude of oxidative bursts [(Suzuki et al., 2011)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pbi.2011.07.014).

Animals use mitochondria and NADPH oxidases. Calcium entry increases the rate of electron flow and drives superoxide and hydrogen peroxide release from the electron transport chain, producing the same ROS species through the same biochemical sequence [(Wu et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41401-025-01576-w).

The chronic activation ring around foreign objects in animals recruits these same ROS amplification systems, while gall tissue uses RBOH to accomplish the same task in plants. Both activation rings therefore rely on homologous calcium linked oxidizing machinery.

## Beneficial ROS and the Threshold to Injury

ROS regulate development, patterning, stress adaptation, and hormone signaling in both kingdoms [(Noctor et al., 2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.semcdb.2017.07.013). Once ROS exceed antioxidant capacity the same biochemical damage occurs across plants and animals. Excessive ROS cause lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, DNA breaks, membrane destabilization, and mitochondrial dysfunction [(Sharma et al., 2012)](https://doi.org/10.1155/2012/217037); [(Das and Roychoudhury, 2014)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fenvs.2014.00053).

This threshold is crossed in both kingdoms during persistent foreign object containment. In animals the inflammatory ring produces chronic ROS. In plants the gall tissue produces chronic ROS. In both cases the containment architecture itself becomes the site where ROS exceed tolerance.

Drought, salinity, heat, and cold intensify ROS beyond safe limits in plants [(Hasanuzzaman et al., 2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21228695); [(Xie et al., 2019)](https://doi.org/10.1155/2019/9732325). Mental, metabolic, and inflammatory stress do the same in animals [(Khan et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.14336/AD.2024.0125-1). The ROS threshold that separates adaptation from injury is therefore identical across kingdoms.

## Ligand Amplification by Auxin and Quinolinic Acid

Auxin in plants and quinolinic acid in animals act as ligand amplifiers that intensify excitatory activation in the presence of persistent foreign objects.

In animals, chronic inflammation around unremovable irritants drives quinolinic acid synthesis. Quinolinic acid activates NMDA receptors, increases calcium influx, and elevates ROS generation, establishing a chronic excitatory zone within the inflammatory ring.

In plants, galling increases auxin concentration around the foreign presence. Auxin increases GLR calcium activation and stimulates RBOH dependent ROS production, establishing a chronic excitatory zone within the gall structure [(Gokce et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10265-024-01528-1).

Auxin and quinolinic acid therefore perform parallel roles within parallel structures. Both amplify calcium signaling. Both escalate ROS. Both strengthen the activation ring around the intruder. Both push the tissue toward the glycolytic threshold.

---

## Glycolytic Shift in Both Kingdoms

When ROS exceed mitochondrial tolerance in either kingdom, the cell shifts toward glycolysis as a protective measure to reduce oxidative load.

In animals, this shift defines the early tumor microenvironment and occurs precisely within the inflammatory containment ring where ROS are highest. In plants, excessive ROS under salinity, drought, wounding, or galling suppress oxidative phosphorylation and push cells toward glycolytic pathways [(Hasanuzzaman et al., 2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21228695). These glycolytic regions occur within the gall, in the same spatial relationship as tumor margins around chronic inflammatory sites in animals.

Galls in plants and tumors in animals represent parallel hypergrowth structures formed by this glycolytic adaptation under persistent ligand driven activation and foreign object stress.

---

## Programmed Cell Death Parallels

When compensatory pathways fail, both kingdoms invoke ROS mediated programmed cell death. In plants, high ROS induce mitochondrial failure, chloroplast injury, cardiolipin oxidation, and cytochrome c release [(Petrov et al., 2015)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2015.00069). In animals, excitotoxic activation causes the same mitochondrial collapse and ROS triggered death pathways [(Wu et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41401-025-01576-w).

Both have identical failure modes when oxidative load at the containment boundary overwhelms repair capacity.

## Unified View

Across all reviewed literature the same biological logic is present. Stress increases calcium signaling and ROS generation in plants and animals. Foreign objects produce chronic activation that cannot resolve. Both kingdoms build containment rings around unremovable objects. Auxin in plant galls and quinolinic acid in animal inflammation perform the same ligand amplification role. Both kingdoms generate superoxide and hydrogen peroxide through calcium linked oxidases and mitochondria. Both cross a redox threshold that forces a glycolytic shift to preserve survival. Both form hypergrowth structures under chronic ligand driven activation, galls in plants and tumors in animals. Both invoke ROS mediated cell death when this fails.

This establishes a single conserved excitatory redox mechanism across the plant and animal kingdoms.
