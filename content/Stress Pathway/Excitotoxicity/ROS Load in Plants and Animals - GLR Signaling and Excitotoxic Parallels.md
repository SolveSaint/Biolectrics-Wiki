## Overview

Plants and animals operate on the same excitation to redox architecture. Both kingdoms generate reactive oxygen species through stress and through persistent foreign object intrusion, and both produce the same ROS species through identical calcium linked biochemical logic.

Stress of every type increases intracellular calcium and ROS production. In animals, psychological, metabolic, inflammatory, thermal, or toxic load enhances glutamate receptor activity, increases calcium influx, and elevates mitochondrial production of superoxide and hydrogen peroxide [(Khan et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.14336/AD.2024.0125-1); [(Wu et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41401-025-01576-w). In plants, drought, salinity, heat, cold, and xenobiotic stress activate GLR channels and RBOH enzymes, raising superoxide and hydrogen peroxide through calcium dependent oxidation of NADPH [(Marino et al., 2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tplants.2011.10.001); [(Suzuki et al., 2011)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pbi.2011.07.014); [(Das and Roychoudhury, 2014)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fenvs.2014.00053); [(Xie et al., 2019)](https://doi.org/10.1155/2019/9732325). Both kingdoms therefore produce identical ROS species through the same calcium coupled pathways, creating a shared baseline of stress induced oxidative activity.

Persistent foreign objects create the second route, and the parallel between kingdoms is exact. In animals, macrophages surround an unremovable object such as asbestos, silica, or a persistent microbial fragment. They attempt to engulf it but fail, which creates a stable inflammatory ring of activated cells around the object. This ring produces continuous ROS and drives the kynurenine pathway toward quinolinic acid, a strong NMDA receptor agonist that amplifies calcium influx and intensifies oxidative load.

In plants, insects, nematodes, microbes, or injected effectors remain embedded in tissue and cannot be removed. The surrounding plant cells form a gall that encloses the intruder, creating a structural ring of activated tissue that directly parallels the inflammatory ring in animals. This gall tissue produces elevated auxin, which amplifies GLR mediated calcium signaling and drives RBOH dependent ROS production [(Hasanuzzaman et al., 2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21228695); [(Noctor et al., 2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.semcdb.2017.07.013). The functional architecture is the same. Both systems build a containment structure around the foreign object, both structures maintain chronic excitatory activation, and both raise ROS through identical calcium linked mechanisms.

Quinolinic acid in animals and auxin in plants are therefore ligand amplifiers that perform equivalent functions. Both raise excitatory signaling, increase calcium throughput, intensify ROS generation, and push tissue toward oxidative strain. When this excitatory load exceeds mitochondrial tolerance, both kingdoms adopt a glycolytic survival strategy. In animals this produces the early tumor microenvironment [(Khan et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.14336/AD.2024.0125-1). In plants this produces gall tissue and other hyperproliferative responses under chronic stress [(Hasanuzzaman et al., 2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21228695). Tumors and galls are parallel hypergrowth structures stabilized by identical redox and ligand driven processes.

This establishes a single conserved excitatory redox mechanism across plants and animals.

## GLR and Glutamate Receptor Parallels

GLR channels in plants and NMDA and AMPA receptors in animals are functional equivalents. Both mediate calcium entry during activation and link excitation to ROS generation.

In plants, GLR channels regulate hydrogen peroxide signaling during cold and salt stress and support acclimation through calcium dependent redox activity [(Li et al., 2019)](https://doi.org/10.1111/pce.13623). GLR mediated nitric oxide signaling also preserves antioxidant balance under salt stress, and blockade of GLRs reduces ROS accumulation under these conditions [(Gokce et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10265-024-01528-1).

In animals, glutamate receptor activation increases calcium influx, drives mitochondrial respiration, and generates superoxide and hydrogen peroxide during excitatory states [(Khan et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.14336/AD.2024.0125-1); [(Wu et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41401-025-01576-w). The structural and functional mapping between GLRs and glutamate receptors defines a direct plant to animal correspondence in excitatory redox coupling.

## RBOH and Mitochondrial ROS Amplification

Plants and animals use similar machinery to amplify ROS once calcium rises.

Plants use respiratory burst oxidase homolog enzymes. These enzymes convert NADPH into superoxide and hydrogen peroxide in a calcium dependent manner and act as central ROS amplifiers in all major stress responses [(Marino et al., 2012)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tplants.2011.10.001). RBOH integrates phosphorylation and calcium inputs to regulate the magnitude of oxidative bursts [(Suzuki et al., 2011)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pbi.2011.07.014).

Animals use mitochondria and NADPH oxidases. Calcium entry increases the rate of electron flow and drives superoxide and hydrogen peroxide release from the electron transport chain, producing the same ROS species through the same biochemical sequence [(Khan et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.14336/AD.2024.0125-1); [(Wu et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41401-025-01576-w).

Both kingdoms therefore produce identical oxidants through homologous calcium linked activation systems.

## Beneficial ROS and the Threshold to Injury

ROS regulate development, patterning, stress adaptation, and hormone signaling in both kingdoms [(Noctor et al., 2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.semcdb.2017.07.013). Once ROS exceed antioxidant capacity the same biochemical damage occurs across plants and animals. Excessive ROS cause lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, DNA breaks, membrane destabilization, and mitochondrial dysfunction [(Sharma et al., 2012)](https://doi.org/10.1155/2012/217037); [(Das and Roychoudhury, 2014)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fenvs.2014.00053). Drought, salinity, heat, cold, and other environmental pressures intensify ROS beyond safe limits in plants [(Hasanuzzaman et al., 2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21228695); [(Xie et al., 2019)](https://doi.org/10.1155/2019/9732325). Psychological, metabolic, inflammatory, thermal, and toxic loads do the same in animals [(Khan et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.14336/AD.2024.0125-1); [(Wu et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41401-025-01576-w).

The ROS threshold that separates adaptation from injury is therefore identical across kingdoms.

## Transgenerational Redox Inheritance in Plants and Animals

The excitatory redox architecture is not only conserved in mechanism, it is also conserved in inheritance. Stress history in parents reshapes methylation at stress regulatory genes and shifts excitability in offspring in both plants and animals.

In plants, repeated environmental stress alters ROS metabolism and redox homeostasis through persistent changes in ROS production, antioxidant capacity, and stress signaling networks [(Hasanuzzaman et al., 2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21228695); [(Noctor et al., 2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.semcdb.2017.07.013); [(Xie et al., 2019)](https://doi.org/10.1155/2019/9732325). These reviews describe stress memory in which prior exposure leaves ROS pathways and defense genes in a primed state that responds faster and more strongly to subsequent stress. DNA methylation and chromatin remodeling at stress genes preserve this configuration across development, and in many cases across generations, so that offspring begin life with altered ROS thresholds and antioxidant set points.

In animals, an equivalent pattern is seen at glucocorticoid regulatory genes. Systematic analysis shows that DNA methylation at NR3C1, FKBP5, and related loci shapes cortisol responses and stress reactivity [(Balfour et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.10.23.25338702). Integrative epigenetic work highlights that parental trauma and chronic load induce directional methylation changes at these genes, and that offspring frequently exhibit a complementary pattern, such as hypermethylation in parents and relative hypomethylation in children at specific FKBP5 regions [(Banushi et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms26073075). This flip is interpreted as a biological accommodation in which offspring adjust glucocorticoid sensitivity and excitatory capacity in response to parental history.

Intermediate animal models show the same logic. In Pekin ducks, parental intake of a high carotenoid orange corn diet, which alters antioxidant and redox status, changes glucocorticoid and fear responses in F1 offspring without affecting growth [(Oluwagbenga et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.psj.2025.105838). This demonstrates that parental redox environment and hormone regulation tune offspring stress reactivity and excitability even when gross morphology is unchanged.

Across these systems, stress leaves a methylation based record on ROS and hormone regulatory genes. That record is passed to offspring as a shifted excitatory baseline, producing hyperexcitable plant lines and stress sensitive or stress buffered animal lineages. The same calcium to ROS architecture is preserved, but the inherited methylation pattern sets how early and how strongly it engages.

## Ligand Amplification by Auxin and Quinolinic Acid

Auxin in plants and quinolinic acid in animals act as ligand amplifiers that intensify excitatory activation in the presence of persistent foreign objects.

In animals, chronic inflammation around unremovable irritants drives quinolinic acid synthesis through the kynurenine pathway. Quinolinic acid activates NMDA receptors, increases calcium influx, and elevates ROS generation, establishing a chronic excitatory microenvironment that precedes glycolytic survival [(Khan et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.14336/AD.2024.0125-1).

In plants, galling increases auxin concentration around the foreign presence. Auxin increases GLR calcium activation and stimulates RBOH dependent ROS production, creating the same state of chronic excitatory load seen in animal inflammation [(Hasanuzzaman et al., 2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21228695); [(Noctor et al., 2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.semcdb.2017.07.013); [(Gokce et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10265-024-01528-1).

Auxin and quinolinic acid therefore perform parallel roles. Both provide ligand driven amplification of calcium signaling, ROS escalation, and proliferative pressure in tissues that are already primed by stress and inherited methylation states.

## Glycolytic Shift in Both Kingdoms

When ROS exceed mitochondrial tolerance in either kingdom, the cell shifts toward glycolysis as a protective measure to reduce oxidative load.

In animals, this shift defines the early tumor microenvironment and occurs in regions of chronic inflammation and excitatory stress [(Khan et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.14336/AD.2024.0125-1). Cells downregulate oxidative phosphorylation, upregulate glycolysis and glutaminolysis, and rely on enhanced antioxidant systems to survive persistent ROS pressure.

In plants, excessive ROS under salinity, drought, wounding, cold, or galling suppress oxidative phosphorylation and push cells toward glycolytic and fermentative pathways to maintain ATP while limiting additional mitochondrial ROS [(Hasanuzzaman et al., 2020)](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijms21228695); [(Hossain and Dietz, 2016)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2016.00548). Galls in plants and tumors in animals represent parallel hypergrowth structures formed by this glycolytic adaptation under persistent ligand driven activation and foreign object stress.

## Programmed Cell Death Parallels

When compensatory pathways fail, both kingdoms invoke ROS mediated programmed cell death. In plants, high ROS induce mitochondrial failure, chloroplast injury, cardiolipin oxidation, and cytochrome c release, followed by execution of programmed cell death pathways [(Petrov et al., 2015)](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpls.2015.00069); [(Sharma et al., 2012)](https://doi.org/10.1155/2012/217037). In animals, excitotoxic activation causes the same mitochondrial collapse, cardiolipin oxidation, cytochrome c release, and ROS triggered death pathways [(Khan et al., 2024)](https://doi.org/10.14336/AD.2024.0125-1); [(Wu et al., 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41401-025-01576-w).

Programmed cell death in plants and apoptosis or necrotic degeneration in animals therefore share the same ROS linked mechanism once antioxidant defenses and metabolic shifts can no longer contain oxidative load.

## Unified View

Across all reviewed literature the same biological logic is present. Stress of many forms increases calcium signaling and ROS generation in plants and animals. Foreign objects produce chronic activation that cannot resolve. Auxin and quinolinic acid act as ligand amplifiers that intensify excitatory load around these objects. Both kingdoms generate superoxide and hydrogen peroxide through calcium linked oxidases and mitochondria. Both cross a redox threshold that forces a glycolytic shift to preserve survival. Both form hypergrowth structures under chronic ligand driven activation, galls in plants and tumors in animals. Both invoke ROS mediated cell death when this fails.

On top of this shared acute and chronic behavior, both kingdoms record these excitatory histories as methylation changes at key regulatory genes. Plants encode stress memory in ROS and antioxidant pathways, producing primed lines and progeny with shifted ROS thresholds. Animals encode stress history in methylation at NR3C1, FKBP5, and related loci, and in experimental systems such as ducks, parental redox and hormone status shape offspring glucocorticoid and fear responses. The combined evidence supports a single conserved excitatory redox mechanism that operates across plant and animal kingdoms and across generations.
